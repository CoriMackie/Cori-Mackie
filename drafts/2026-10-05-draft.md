### Research Question

> Does the price Mahi Pono pays for East Maui water reflect what that water is worth, and what does Maui give up by allocating it this way?

## 1. Introduction and Position

In 1869, what would become, one of the big five ag companies, Alexander & Baldwin purchased 12 acres of arid land in Central Maui, followed by another 559 acres the next year to establish a sugarcane plantation. Recognizing that the land’s agricultural value depended on water, the company helped develop the 17-mile Hāmākua Ditch (Battle Looms, 1997) to divert water from East Maui streams. Over time, the irrigation system expanded to hundreds of intakes, miles of ditches and tunnels, reservoirs, dams, and flumes. Reliable water access made Central Maui agriculture highly productive and helped A&B expand its landholdings. The same land and irrigation system would eventually become central to Mahi Pono’s agricultural operations.

These diversions also reduced water available for East Maui communities and traditional loʻi kalo cultivation. At the time of the Māhele, approximately 500 loʻi were claimed in the area (Battle Looms, 1997), illustrating the importance of stream water to the region’s existing agricultural economy.

In 1876, the government granted access to water for \$100 per year for 20 years. (Battle Looms, 1997) This early arrangement raises an economic question that remains relevant 150 years later: **Does the price paid for access to East Maui water reflect the economic value that water creates?** Without reliable water, much of Central Maui’s arid agricultural land would have considerably less productive value.

Having grown up on Maui and witnessed the decline of large-scale agriculture, the Lahaina fires and the critical role water availability and infrastructure played in the firefighting response, as well as efforts to restore water to traditional Hawaiian farming communities, I am interested in the future of agriculture, water rights, and the equitable and environmentally sustainable use of water. I am also aware that much of Central Maui’s former plantation land requires significant capital investment and reliable water access to remain productive rather than sit unused. I chose this topic to better understand the economics behind Canadian owned Mahi Pono’s agricultural investment and water needs, while considering the competing demands for Maui’s limited water resources rather than assuming either side of the debate is correct.

My hypothesis is that the price paid for access to East Maui water does not fully reflect its economic value. I will test this by examining what Mahi Pono pays for water access, the agricultural value that water supports, and the opportunity costs associated with diverting it. My analysis will apply the economic principles of **scarcity, supply and demand, and opportunity cost**.

## 2. Background: The Permit and Who Holds It

## The Board of Land and Natural Resources (BLNR) sets the price for access to East Maui water through revocable permits that are renewed annually (DLNR, 2025). In June 2025, A&B transferred full ownership of East Maui Irrigation (EMI) to Mahi Pono, and the 2026 permit, which began January 1, 2026, was issued to EMI and Mahi Pono jointly at \$24,306 per month, about \$292,000 per year (DLNR, 2025). The system serves approximately 12,900 acres of Mahi Pono agricultural land while also providing water for Maui County. Mahi Pono reports diverting an average of about 28 million gallons per day from East Maui, of which about 3.4 million gallons go to the county, leaving roughly 24.6 million gallons per day for its farms (Mahi Pono, 2026). In July 2026, a state judge ordered a contested-case hearing on the permit and capped Mahi Pono's diversion at 31.5 million gallons per day until that hearing is held ("Judge Orders Hearing," 2026). In August 2026, BLNR declined to move forward with a proposed 30-year water license, leaving the arrangement under a revocable permit ("BLNR Nixes," 2026). Importantly, Mahi Pono owns the EMI infrastructure, but it does not own the water itself; water remains a public resource regulated by the State.

## 3. Price vs. Value

I use 3% as a conservative benchmark based on the approximately 3% 10-year U.S. Treasury yield in 2018. I also test the calculation at 5% to show how the estimated annual value changes under a higher assumed rate of return.

If the \$62 million is a market price for access, then access is worth about \$1.9–\$3.1 million a year (Alexander & Baldwin, 2019), while the rent is about \$292,000/year (Hawaiʻi Department of Land and Natural Resources \[DLNR\], 2025). The gap is about \$1.6–\$2.8 million a year, or about 6–11 times the rent. The value of the gap is created by a resource that is not produced: the water falls as rain, so Mahi Pono does not produce it. When a resource is priced below its value, the difference is economic rent. The water is public, so the rent is effectively a subsidy from the public to the permit holder. A&B captured it once: in the \$62 million sale to Mahi Pono, a private company was paid for access to water it did not own. Mahi Pono captures it every year through cheap water and through higher land value, since its land has secure water. The public gets around \$290,000 a year.

The \$62 million reflects secure, long-term access. A one-year permit may not fully reflect the economic value of that reliable access.

## 4. Who Pays What

The price of water varies depending on who uses it. Mahi Pono pays approximately 2.5 cents per 1,000 gallons in permit rent. Including the cost of operating and maintaining the EMI system increases its estimated cost to about 26 cents per 1,000 gallons. By comparison, Maui County’s agricultural water rate is \$1.31 per 1,000 gallons.

## 5. What Maui Gives Up

The historical stream-flow data shows that the water supply is not constant. The base flow—the dry-weather groundwater-supported portion of stream flow that remains when rainfall runoff is low—has declined substantially during previous periods. The graph shows a decline of approximately 23% after 1943. More recently, Honopou's 2025 average was its second lowest in the 144-year record, while Maui has continued to experience extremely low stream flows.

This creates an important economic issue because the amount of water available can decline while Mahi Pono’s permitted diversion remains relatively fixed. The current permit allows approximately 31.5 million gallons per day. If base flow were to fall another 23%, similar to the historical decline shown in the graph, but the permitted diversion remained at 31.5 mgd, the diversion would represent a larger share of the remaining water supply. **The permit does not become larger, but its claim on a shrinking resource does.**

The economic concept is scarcity. As the supply of water decreases, the value of each remaining gallon increases. However, the price Mahi Pono pays does not automatically increase as the resource becomes scarcer. This means that the economic cost of scarcity falls more heavily on uses outside the permit: kalo farmers, downstream farmers, stream ecosystems, and other community users. This is part of the opportunity cost of allocating a large fixed quantity of water to one permit holder.

There is also a longer-term opportunity cost. Water left in the stream preserves future choices. Once a stream stops flowing, some environmental effects may be difficult to reverse. Allocating water today therefore has a value beyond its immediate agricultural use because maintaining stream flow preserves the option to use that resource differently in the future.

There are limitations to this analysis. The 23% scenario is based on a historical change and is **not a prediction that stream flow will fall another 23%**. Stream flow is affected by rainfall, drought cycles, El Niño and other climate conditions, groundwater recharge, and changes in measurement and diversion practices. The scenario instead demonstrates the economic consequence of a fixed diversion when the underlying resource becomes scarcer.

## 6. Mahi Pono’s Best Case

Mahi Pono took over agricultural land that had been largely dormant after the end of sugar production and had no clear agricultural future. They have invested in finding crops that can be successfully grown in Central Maui, while providing agricultural jobs and working toward increasing Hawaiʻi’s local food production. The EMI system also continues to provide water that serves Maui County users. In addition, Mahi Pono is now responsible for maintaining and operating a large and aging irrigation system. In 2025, approximately \$139,000 was spent on watershed management, while the total cost associated with operating EMI has been estimated at approximately \$3 million annually. These costs are important because the approximately \$292,000 permit rent alone does not represent Mahi Pono’s full cost of accessing the water.

## 7. Recommendation

My recommendation is for the State to develop a new economic model for East Maui water rather than continuing to rely on a system that was established long before Mahi Pono existed. The original system was developed primarily to support large-scale commercial agriculture, not to serve Maui's present-day population or the competing needs that exist today. Mahi Pono inherited this system; it did not create it. However, Maui has changed, and the economic model governing the water should change with it.

In the short term, Mahi Pono should pay a higher amount for access that better reflects the economic value and increasing scarcity of the water. In the longer term, both the price and amount available for diversion should respond to changes in water supply and recognize other uses, including residential needs, small farms, kalo cultivation, stream health, and future needs.

Mahi Pono has a legitimate economic interest in maintaining reliable water access because it supports its agricultural operations and preserves the productive value of its land. The State, however, has a broader responsibility because the water is a public resource. A new model should therefore allow agriculture to remain economically viable while making sure that the value and scarcity of the water are not determined
